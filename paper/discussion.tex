\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
 
Our results show that \textsc{Kaiburr} remains computationally practical despite much higher security margins. On all our
benchmarks, every \textsc{Kaiburr} parameter set is substantially faster than FrodoKEM-640. This holds even for our largest
parameter set, kaiburr-6144, which matches FrodoKEM-640's communication cost while reaching 1717-bit classical and 1557-bit
quantum security, and is still roughly 2.5--3 times faster across all operations. In other words, \textsc{Kaiburr}'s
\emph{worst} case is comfortably cheaper than FrodoKEM's \emph{best} case.
 
A gap this wide exemplifies the computational advantage of retaining module structure. It also shows that the bandwidth
separating the two schemes can be spent in more than one way. Even the largest ML-KEM parameter set, ML-KEM-1024, sits 8,048
public-key bytes and 8,152 ciphertext bytes below the smallest FrodoKEM parameter set, FrodoKEM-640. FrodoKEM spends that
bandwidth on \emph{removing} algebraic structure; \textsc{Kaiburr} spends it on \emph{retaining the structure} while reaching the
security estimates above.
 
Framed this way, our comparison also raises a practical question: if FrodoKEM-640's communication and computational costs are
already practical enough for conservative deployments, why not spend the same budget on increasing the security margin of ML-KEM? Our
results suggest that this is a plausible alternative. Naively increasing the module rank does reach a correctness limit, but
with \textsc{Kaiburr}'s modified noise distribution, the same bandwidth budget supports much larger module ranks, much higher
security estimates, and up to three times the speed. What the comparison exposes, then, is a tradeoff between two forms of
conservatism: removing algebraic structure, or retaining it and spending the resources on security margin instead.
 
Against HQC the picture is different, since HQC hedges against a different unknown. On cost alone, however, \textsc{Kaiburr}
compares favorably in terms of performance. kaiburr-1792 is smaller than HQC-1 in combined pk+ct size and
faster than HQC-5 in every operation on both machines, despite security estimates far beyond the category-5 target. HQC's
advantage is concentrated in key generation, where every HQC parameter set beats the corresponding \textsc{Kaiburr}
parameter set by a wide margin. On efficiency grounds, therefore, the margin hedge and the diversity hedge are not in
tension, and a deployment that wants both can afford both.
 
Scaling the module rank also changes where the time goes, and our ACC results show how. Matrix generation accounts for
73--84\% of total cycles, and its cost grows quadratically with $k$, since a $k \times k$ matrix requires $k^2$ sampled
polynomials. Hardware SHAKE-128 acceleration does not remove this bottleneck since the core waits on the accelerator for only
5.5--6.2\% of total cycles, with little change across parameter sets. The software rejection-sampling loop grows to
69.9\% of total cycles for kaiburr-6144. For an accelerated implementation, the optimization target then moves from
symmetric throughput to rejection sampling. This is consistent with prior work by Abdulrahman et al., who also identify
rejection sampling as a target for further hardware optimization~\cite{SP:AOPPSS25}; in our setting, its cost grows with $k$
until it dominates matrix generation.
 
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, our work explicitly explores increasing the security margin as a hedge against improvements
in the best known attacks today. By this, we mean that \textsc{Kaiburr} is and will remain vulnerable to structural attacks that exploit the 
module structure, a class of attacks FrodoKEM is immune against. Such a breakthrough would still motivate an unstructured scheme, so the two
approaches remain complementary rather than substitutes. In principle, they could even be combined in a hybrid
construction, deriving the final key from both a high-margin module KEM such as \textsc{Kaiburr} and an unstructured LWE KEM
such as FrodoKEM. Such a construction would combine the security-margin and structural-diversity hedges, at the cost of
increased communication and computation.
 
The purpose of designing and evaluating these high security variants of ML-KEM is not to argue that such security margins
are currently necessary. Considering the relative stability of lattice cryptanalysis and security estimates in recent years, we do not expect improvements of
the magnitude that would require such parameter sets in the near future. What we characterize instead is how much margin ML-KEM can 
accommodate and it costs to obtain it, so that the tradeoff is already understood if it ever becomes necessary.
 
Two directions follow from this work. Our noise family $f(t)$ is not specific to ML-KEM, so it is an open question whether
the same construction could extend the security margin of other module-LWE schemes, such as ML-DSA. On the implementation
side, our ACC results leave dedicated hardware support for rejection sampling as the natural next step, since that is what
scaling $k$ makes the bottleneck.
