\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results show that with the proposed generalization of the noise distribution used in ML-KEM, 
we can increase the module rank of ML-KEM and thereby obtain a massive security margin to hedge
against possible future improvements in solving (M)LWE. The performance of the largest
parameter set we propose, \kaiburr-6144 reaches Core-SVP estimates of 1717 bits classical and 1557 bits quantum,
while still outperforming the \emph{smallest} parameter set of FrodoKEM in every regard:
it has slightly smaller public keys and ciphertexts and is more than twice as fast.
Also in comparison with HQC, \kaiburr is competitive in performance at a much larger
security margin. 

\begin{quote}
  \emph{What does this mean for deployments of post-quantum cryptography that 
aim for extra protection against possible future advances in cryptanalysis and are 
willing to accept a significant performance decrease compared to, e.g., simply deploying
  ML-KEM-1024?}
\end{quote}

Certainly, \emph{some} deployments are constrained to standardized schemes and parameter
sets, possibly even to specific combiners or non-hybrid deployment. In the following 
discussion, we ignore such constraints imposed by policy or certification requirements
and instead focus purely technical aspects. We note however, that the smallest parameter
set of FrodoKEM, which inspires our performance limit for \kaiburr, is \emph{not} standardized
by ISO~\cite{San26}.

The first observation is that for any post-quantum KEM at a performance profile in the ballpark of FrodoKEM,
a hybrid combination with classical ECDH should be a no-brainer. To quantify this 
statement, let us consider the performance of the NIST level-3 parameter set of FrodoKEM
and of X25519 ECDH on the Intel Coffee Lake microarchitecture (cf. \cref{tab:8700k}). 
Median cycles for FrodoKEM key generation are 8270912, for encapsulation 8528877,
and for decapsulation 8411864. 
According to SUPERCOP~\cite{XXX}, X25519 takes 27345 median cycles for
key generation and 83438 cycles for shared-key computation on the same
microarchitecture (specifically, on an Intel Xeon E-2124), so adding those
cycles to FrodoKEM costs less than 2\% overhead (plus the cost of
hashing incurred by the combiner). In terms of increase to public-key and
ciphertext sizes the argument is even more obvious: adding 32 bytes to
objects of more than 15\,KB should not be noticeable in pretty much any
application. Given the remarkably stable security track record of ECC,
this hybrid is very likely to protect ciphertexts until the arrival of
large universal quantum computers, even if lattice cryptanalysis makes
unexpected progress to the extent that FrodoKEM ciphertexts are no longer
secure.

A slightly less obvious recommendation is to always combine FrodoKEM 
with at least a standardized parameter set of ML-KEM. As ML-KEM offers
roughly comparable (in fact, somewhat better) performance as X25519,
the argument for computational performance increase is similar as for
X25519. With regards to increase in ciphertext and public-key size, the overhead
becomes noticeable, but remains solidly at single-digit percentages.
One might think that such a hybrid does not actually add much in terms
of security, as a break of FrodoKEM is fairly likely to also affect
ML-KEM. However, this is not true for software bugs and implementation attacks, 
which may well affect only one of the two schemes. This is particularly
true as implementation security of post-quantum schemes is more complex
and by far not as mature as for for ECC.  Hence, adding ML-KEM
to FrodoKEM offers additional \emph{post-quantum} security at a very low cost.

Even more importantly, and this is the central messages of this paper,
our results call into question the use of high-security parameter sets of FrodoKEM: 
A combination of FrodoKEM-640 and \kaiburr-6144 offers significantly better performance than FrodoKEM-976
both in terms of cycle counts and in terms of ciphertext and public-key sizes.

\kaiburr hedge simultanuously against even massive improvements in attacks

against any improvements in attacks that only apply to structured lattices 
(through the use of FrodoKEM). The only advantage of a higher-security FrodoKEM
variant would only come into play if both structured lattices are completely
broken and attacks against unstructured lattices improve significantly, but
not by too much (i.e., within the slightly increased margin of the larger
FrodoKEM parameters). Additionally, a combination of \kaiburr and FrodoKEM
has the same benefits of some additional hedging against implementation
attacks that standalone FrodoKEM is lacking.

A gap this wide reflects the computational advantage of retaining module structure. It also shows that the bandwidth separating the two schemes can be
spent in more than one way. Even ML-KEM-1024, the largest ML-KEM parameter set, has a public key 8,048 bytes and a ciphertext 8,152 bytes smaller than
FrodoKEM-640, the smallest FrodoKEM parameter set. FrodoKEM spends that bandwidth on \emph{removing} algebraic structure, whereas \textsc{Kaiburr} spends it on
a larger security margin while \emph{retaining} the structure. Framed this way, our work also raises a practical question for deployment.
FrodoKEM-640's communication and computational costs are already considered acceptable for conservative deployments, such as those following the
recommendations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. Could a deployment that accepts these costs instead spend them on a high-margin
variant of ML-KEM? Our work suggests that it can. Naively increasing the module rank reaches a correctness limit (Section~\ref{sec:warmup}), but with \textsc{Kaiburr}'s noise distribution, the same budget supports
module ranks up to 24 and much higher security estimates, while every parameter set remains faster than FrodoKEM-640. Notably, the budget
can also be split between the two by using a hybrid of \kaiburr-1792 and FrodoKEM-976, which has a smaller public key and ciphertext than 
FrodoKEM-1344 (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).

We find it important to stress that these two approaches remain complementary rather than substitutes, since they
hedge against different hypothetical attacks. 
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, \textsc{Kaiburr}'s larger margin is a hedge against improvements to the best known lattice attacks
and it offers no protection against attacks that exploit module structure, which by design do
not apply to FrodoKEM. Such an attack would still motivate an unstructured scheme. This is also what makes splitting the budget worthwhile as the hybrid of
\kaiburr-1792 and FrodoKEM-976 combines a large security margin with resistance to structural attacks. Compared with
FrodoKEM-1344, it has a much higher lattice attack cost and, based on its component costs, is faster.

HQC hedges against yet another possibility, a failure of the LWE assumption itself, so the comparison with HQC is one of cost only. \kaiburr-1792 is
smaller than HQC-1 in combined public-key and ciphertext size and faster than HQC-5 in every operation on all three machines. For the larger parameter sets,
HQC-3 and HQC-5 are faster than \kaiburr-4608 and \kaiburr-6144 in key generation and encapsulation, while decapsulation is comparable or faster for
\textsc{Kaiburr}. Since their costs are of the same order, the choice between \textsc{Kaiburr} and HQC comes down to whether one believes improvements in lattice
attacks are more likely than a break of the LWE assumption itself.

An important consequence of changing the module rank is that it also changes where the cost goes, especially once Keccak, which is typically the 
dominant cost of ML-KEM in software, is accelerated. In ML-KEM, only two steps scale quadratically with $k$. The first is generating the $k \times k$ matrix
$\mathbf{A}$, which requires SHAKE-128 output and rejection sampling for $k^2$ polynomials. The second is the matrix--vector product in the NTT domain, which 
requires $k^2$ base multiplications. All other steps, including noise sampling, the NTTs, and hashing, scale linearly~\cite{fips203}. Our ACC
profiling results demonstrate this concretely. Matrix generation accounts for 73--84\% of total cycles and the base multiplication for 8--10\%, while 
the share of the noise sampler falls from 6.3\% to 2.1\%. Hardware SHAKE-128 acceleration does not remove this bottleneck either. The core waits on the 
accelerator for only 5.5--6.2\% of total cycles, with little change across parameter sets, while the software rejection-sampling loop grows to 69.9\% of 
total cycles for \kaiburr-6144. For an accelerated implementation, the optimization target therefore moves from symmetric
throughput to rejection sampling. This is consistent with prior work that also identifies rejection sampling as a target for further hardware
optimization~\cite{SP:AOPPSS25}. In our setting, we quantify how this dominant cost grows with the module rank, at parameters far
beyond those commonly studied for ML-KEM.

Our results also have several other limitations. The security figures are estimates; for \kaiburr-4608 and \kaiburr-6144, the cheapest attacks use 
block sizes outside the range in which the MATZOV cost model was derived, and we obtained them with a modified estimator (Section~\ref{sec:distribution}). 
Our decryption-failure target of $2^{-128}$ is less strict than what ML-KEM achieves, and a stricter target would lower the
largest module rank each noise parameter $t$ supports. For the Jasmin
implementation we machine-check constant-time behavior but not functional correctness. The constant-time behavior of the ACC
implementation is also not machine-checked, and the ACC results are for a configuration with enlarged data memory. Finally, the hybrid of
Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} has not been implemented, and its costs are sums of
component measurements.

Several directions follow from this work. Our noise family $f(t)$ is not specific to ML-KEM, so it is an interesting question whether the same
construction could extend the security margin of other module-LWE schemes, such as ML-DSA. On the implementation side, our ACC results make dedicated
hardware support for rejection sampling the natural next step. It also remains open whether scaling beyond $k=29$ is possible by modifying other
parameters of ML-KEM while making minimal changes to existing implementations. A machine-checked functional-correctness and IND-CCA proof for 
\textsc{Kaiburr} extending \texttt{formosa-mlkem}~\cite{fvk} is also in scope. Finally, implementing the hybrid as a single KEM with a concrete combiner, and benchmarking it end to
end, remains to be done.
