\section{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

We benchmark the C and Jasmin implementations on three Intel microarchitectures: Skylake, Coffee Lake, and Raptor Lake, with Hyper-Threading and Turbo 
Boost disabled. For the hybrid Raptor Lake processor, we pin the benchmark to a performance core. All \kaiburr code is compiled using \texttt{GCC 12.2.0}
with the flags \texttt{-O3}, \texttt{-march=native}, and \texttt{-fomit-frame-pointer}.

We compare the \textsc{Kaiburr} variants with FrodoKEM and HQC. We use the SHAKE-based variant of FrodoKEM as the
primary comparison baseline, as both FrodoKEM-SHAKE and \textsc{Kaiburr} use SHAKE-based symmetric primitives.
FrodoKEM-AES can take advantage of dedicated AES instructions on our Intel processors, which introduces an 
implementation-specific hardware advantage unrelated to the lattice-based KEM operations we are looking to compare.\footnote{Additionally,
we do not report a direct cycle-count comparison with KyFrog~\cite{kyfrog}, another high-security KEM introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, since no
public hand-optimized implementation of the scheme exists.}
We mainly compare against FrodoKEM-640, the smallest FrodoKEM parameter set, since it is already substantially
more expensive than \textsc{Kaiburr}. On Coffee Lake, we also report FrodoKEM-976 and FrodoKEM-1344, which we use in
the hybrid comparison of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. For additional context, we also report ML-KEM-1024 performance on this microarchitecture.

All three KEMs are evaluated using a common benchmark driver in which we first perform $1000$ untimed warm-up iterations, 
followed by $10001$ timed iterations. We report the median 
execution time and interquartile range ($Q_1$--$Q_3$). This procedure is repeated three times, and we report the 
results from the run whose median is itself the median of the three runs, together with its corresponding quartiles. 
Each iteration verifies that the decapsulated shared secret matches the encapsulated shared secret.

Randomness comes from Linux \texttt{getrandom}. FrodoKEM and \textsc{Kaiburr} call it within \texttt{keypair} and \texttt{enc}, 
so that syscall is inside the measured region. HQC instead draws randomness from a SHAKE-256 PRNG that the
caller must seed; we seed it once from \texttt{getrandom} before timing begins, so HQC's timings exclude the syscall.
\footnote{Each implementation keeps the
randomness handling of its upstream codebase; we chose not to modify HQC.
This also \emph{slightly} favors HQC in our comparison.}

The benchmark results are reported separately for each processor. Results for the Intel Core i7-6700K,
Intel Core i7-8700K, and Intel Core i9-13900K are given in Tables~\ref{tab:6700k}, \ref{tab:8700k}, and
\ref{tab:13900k}, respectively.

Our benchmarks show that \textsc{Kaiburr} is faster than FrodoKEM-640 across every
\textbf{operation}, \textbf{parameter set}, \textbf{implementation}, and \textbf{machine}.
 \kaiburr-1792 is $21.5$--$28.0\times$ faster, \kaiburr-4608 is $4.2$--$5.3\times$ faster, and \kaiburr-6144 is
$2.4$--$3.1\times$ faster than FrodoKEM-640.

Which \textsc{Kaiburr} implementation is faster depends on the microarchitecture: Jasmin beats C/asm in every
operation on Skylake and Coffee Lake (by 3.6--13.1\%), while C/asm beats Jasmin in every operation on
Raptor Lake (by 9.2--14.0\%).

From \kaiburr-1792 ($k=7$) to \kaiburr-4608 ($k=18$) to \kaiburr-6144 ($k=24$), C/asm KeyGen grows by
${\approx}5.2\times$ and ${\approx}1.7\times$ on all three machines, which lies between the growth of $k$
($2.57\times$, $1.33\times$) and of $k^2$ ($6.61\times$, $1.78\times$).

On Coffee Lake, \kaiburr-1792 takes $2.5$--$2.6\times$ the cycles of ML-KEM-1024 in every operation, for both
C/asm and Jasmin, which is expected. From $k=4$ to $k=7$, $k$ grows by $1.75\times$ and $k^2$ by
$3.06\times$, and the ratio lies between the two, just as it does across \kaiburr parameter sets.

\kaiburr-1792 is faster than HQC-5 in every operation on every machine (C/asm: 64--90\% fewer cycles).
At matching indices (\kaiburr-1792/HQC-1, \kaiburr-4608/HQC-3, \kaiburr-6144/HQC-5), the comparison
depends on the operation. HQC KeyGen is $1.7$--$3.9\times$ faster, and HQC Enc is $1.6$--$2.0\times$ faster
for the two larger parameter sets. On the other hand, decapsulation, which is HQC's most expensive operation, favors \kaiburr
meaningfully: \kaiburr-1792 is $2.3\times$ faster than HQC-1, \kaiburr-6144 is $1.2\times$ faster than HQC-5, and
\kaiburr-4608 is within 2\% of HQC-3.

Interquartile ranges are below 2\% of the median for every measurement.

We additionally benchmark the ACC implementation on Pavona's cycle-accurate RTL simulator. The simulator is 
deterministic, so repeated runs on the same input are cycle-identical. We therefore run each operation once on a 
fixed input (all-zero randomness) rather than reporting a median and interquartile range. To obtain per-operation costs, 
we build the keypair--encapsulate--decapsulate round trip three times, terminating after KeyGen, after Enc, 
and after Dec, giving total cycle counts $M_1$, $M_2$, $M_3$; then $\mathrm{KeyGen}=M_1$, $\mathrm{Enc}=M_2-M_1$, 
and $\mathrm{Dec}=M_3-M_2$. Results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:acc}.

Table~\ref{tab:acc-profile} breaks down the same round trip using the simulator's per-function and
per-instruction execution statistics. Matrix generation (\texttt{poly\_gen\_matrix}), which expands the
public seed into $\mathbf{A}$ by rejection sampling on SHAKE-128 output, takes 73--84\% of all cycles.
Almost none of this is spent waiting for the hardware SHAKE-128 core, since stalls on
\textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{bn.wsrr}} stay at about 6\% of the total for every parameter set. The cost
is the software rejection-sampling loop itself, namely its mask/compare/select arithmetic
(\textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{bn.and}}, \textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{bn.cmp}}, \textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{bn.sel}})
and the accept/reject branches (\textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{bne}}, \textcolor{hotpink}{\texttt{beq}}).
