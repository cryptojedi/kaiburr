\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

ML-KEM\footnote{We use \textsc{Kyber} and ML-KEM
interchangeably, except when separating the NIST submission from the standardized version.}~\cite{fips203} is built in two layers. 
At its core is an IND-CPA-secure public-key encryption scheme, K-PKE, whose security reduces to module-learning-with-errors (MLWE). ML-KEM uses a variant
of the Fujisaki--Okamoto (FO) transform to lift K-PKE to an IND-CCA2-secure KEM where decapsulation decrypts, re-encrypts the recovered message with the derived
randomness, and returns a pseudorandom key if the result differs from the received ciphertext. In this work, we leave the
FO transform and the symmetric primitives unchanged, so all of our changes are confined to K-PKE, which we briefly review in this section. 
We refer to~\cite{fips203} for the full specification.

\paragraph{Ring and module setup.} Let $n=256$ and $q=3329$, and let $\Rq=\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$ be \textsc{Kyber}'s
base ring, with coefficients centered in $(-q/2,q/2]$. The modulus satisfies $n \mid (q-1)$, which enables the number-theoretic
transform (NTT) used for multiplication in $\Rq$. A module rank $k$ determines
the dimensions of the public matrix $\mathbf{A}\in \Rq^{k\times k}$ and of the secret and error vectors in $\Rq^k$.
$k=2,3,4$ give ML-KEM-512, -768, and -1024, respectively~\cite{fips203}.

\paragraph{K-PKE.} Let $\chi$ be a noise distribution over $\Rq$ with small coefficients, instantiated below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Key generation:} expand a public $32$-byte seed $\rho$ into $\mathbf{A}\in \Rq^{k\times k}$ using SHAKE-128,
    directly in the NTT domain. Sample $\mathbf{s},\mathbf{e}\leftarrow\chi^k$ and set $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e}$.
    The public key is $(\mathbf{t},\rho)$ and the secret key is $\mathbf{s}$.
  \item \emph{Encryption} of a message $m\in\{0,1\}^{n}$, viewed as a polynomial with binary coefficients: sample
    $\mathbf{r}\leftarrow\chi^k$, $\mathbf{e}_1\leftarrow\chi^k$, and $e_2\leftarrow\chi$, and compute
    $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_1$ and $v=\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{r}+e_2+\lceil q/2\rfloor\cdot m$.
  \item \emph{Decryption:} compute $v-\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{u}$ and decode each coefficient to $1$ if it is closer to
    $\lceil q/2\rfloor$ than to $0$, and to $0$ otherwise.
\end{itemize}
Expanding the decryption step gives $v-\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{u}=\lceil q/2\rfloor\cdot m+\tilde{n}$ with noise term
$$\tilde{n} = \mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{e}_1 + e_2 .$$

\paragraph{Centered binomial noise.} In ML-KEM, $\chi$ samples each coefficient from the centered binomial distribution
$\mathrm{CBD}_\eta$, which draws $2\eta$ uniform bits $a_1,\dots,a_\eta,b_1,\dots,b_\eta$ and outputs
$\sum_{i=1}^\eta a_i - \sum_{i=1}^\eta b_i$, a value in $\{-\eta,\ldots,\eta\}$ with variance $\eta/2$. ML-KEM uses $\eta_1$ for
$\mathbf{s},\mathbf{e}$, and $\mathbf{r}$, and $\eta_2$ for $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $e_2$; ML-KEM-768 and -1024 use $\eta_1=\eta_2=2$,
and ML-KEM-512 uses $\eta_1=3$ and $\eta_2=2$. $\mathrm{CBD}_\eta$ is especially desirable because it can be sampled from raw PRF
output in constant time without rejection, using only bit-counting and a subtraction. \textsc{Kaiburr} replaces it with the noise
family of Section~\ref{sec:distribution}.

\paragraph{Decryption failure.} Decryption is correct as long as every coefficient of $\tilde{n}$ is smaller than $q/4$ in absolute value.
We refer to the decryption-failure probability $\delta$ as the probability, over the randomness of key generation and
encryption, that at least one of the $n$ coefficients of $\tilde{n}$ exceeds this threshold\footnote{It is well established that once
an attacker finds a single failure, they can use it as a compass to drastically accelerate finding more failures. This type of attack
is known as ``directional failure-boosting''~\cite{EC:DAnRosVir20}.}.
To compute $\delta$, we adapt the \textsc{Kyber} team's scripts~\cite{kyber-scripts}. Each coefficient of
$\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{r}$ and of $\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{e}_1$ is a signed sum of $kn$ products of two independent noise coefficients.
The scripts compute the distribution of a single coefficient of $\tilde{n}$ by convolution, take its tail mass beyond $q/4$,
and multiply by $n$ (a union bound over the coefficients).

\paragraph{Ciphertext compression.} Instead of transmitting $\mathbf{u}$ and $v$ at full $12$-bit precision, ML-KEM compresses them by
rounding each coefficient to $d_u$ and $d_v$ bits, respectively, with $(d_u,d_v)=(10,4)$ for ML-KEM-512 and -768 and $(11,5)$ for
ML-KEM-1024. This reduces the ciphertext from $384(k+1)$ to $32(d_u k+d_v)$ bytes, e.g.\ from $1536$ to $1088$ bytes for ML-KEM-768.
The receiver decompresses to $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{c}_u$ and $v+c_v$, where $\mathbf{c}_u$ and $c_v$ are the rounding errors, so the
noise term becomes
$$\tilde{n} = \mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{e}_1 - \mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{c}_u + e_2 + c_v ,$$
trading a higher $\delta$ for a smaller ciphertext. Throughout this work, ``ciphertext compression'' refers to this rounding of $\mathbf{u}$ and $v$.

\paragraph{Module-LWE.} The decisional Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) problem~\cite{DCC:LanSte15} over $\Rq$ asks an adversary
to distinguish $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e})$, where $\mathbf{A}\leftarrow \Rq^{\ell\times k}$ is uniform and
$\mathbf{s}\leftarrow \chi^k$, $\mathbf{e}\leftarrow \chi^\ell$, from a uniformly random pair in $\Rq^{\ell\times k}\times \Rq^\ell$.
The IND-CPA security of K-PKE reduces to MLWE with $\chi$. The public key is an instance with $\ell=k$, and, with $\mathbf{t}$ replaced
by a uniform vector, the ciphertext is an instance with $\ell=k+1$. Increasing $k$ increases the dimension $kn$ of the underlying lattice
problem and is the main lever \textsc{Kyber} uses to raise its claimed security level~\cite{kyberround1}.

\paragraph{Core-SVP hardness estimates.} We estimate MLWE hardness with the standard Core-SVP methodology~\cite{USENIX:ADPS16}
used throughout the \textsc{Kyber} submission documents and implemented in the \textsc{Kyber} team's public estimator
scripts~\cite{kyber-scripts}. The adversary runs BKZ with block size $b$ in either the primal or the dual attack, and the cost of
BKZ-$b$ is approximated by a single SVP call in dimension $b$, estimated as $2^{cb}$ operations with $c\approx 0.292$ for classical
sieving~\cite{SODA:BDGL16} and $c\approx 0.265$ for quantum sieving~\cite{laarhoven-thesis}. For each parameter set, the scripts
search over $b$ and the number of samples $\ell$ for the cheapest successful attack, primal or dual, and report its $\log_2$ cost as
the \emph{classical} and \emph{quantum} Core-SVP security level. They depend on the noise distribution only through its standard
deviation. For reference, the same scripts give classical/quantum levels of $117/106$, $181/164$, and $253/230$ for ML-KEM-512,
-768, and -1024, respectively.

\paragraph{Lattice estimator.} We cross-check the numbers from the \textsc{Kyber} Core-SVP scripts with the lattice estimator~\cite{estimator}, which aims to
provide tighter estimates and evaluates a wider range of attacks.\footnote{Forked from
\url{https://github.com/malb/lattice-estimator}, commit \texttt{53da598}.} Its
full estimate costs lattice reduction with the MATZOV model~\cite{matzov2022lwe} by default, rather than with Core-SVP.
We model each \kaiburr parameter set as an LWE instance of dimension $256k$ with $256k$ samples, $q=3329$, and secret and error
coefficients of standard deviation $\sigma(t)$ (Section~\ref{sec:distribution}). For FrodoKEM, we use the estimator's built-in
parameter sets. We keep the estimator's defaults with one exception, explained below.

\paragraph{Raising the block-size limit.} By default, the estimator caps the BKZ block size at $1754$, where the Core-SVP cost
reaches $2^{512}$, and reports an infinite cost for any attack beyond it, so every attack on the two largest \kaiburr parameter sets is
infinite. We raise the cap to $16384$, together with the same hard-coded limit in the primal hybrid and BDD attacks; these are our
only changes to the estimator.
