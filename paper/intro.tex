\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
After a multi-year, multi-round public competition, NIST published its first post-quantum standards FIPS-203~\cite{fips203}, FIPS-204~\cite{fips204},
and FIPS-205~\cite{fips205} in 2024, specifying ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA, respectively, with FN-DSA to follow in FIPS-206~\cite{fips206}.
In 2025, NIST selected an additional KEM, HQC, for standardization in the fourth round~\cite{fourthstatus}. Unlike ML-KEM, which is based on
Module Learning With Errors (MLWE), HQC relies on the Quasi-Cyclic Syndrome Decoding (QCSD) problem~\cite{hqc2025spec}, providing
diversity beyond lattice-based assumptions. Among lattice-based designs, the Round 3 KEMs~\cite{nistpqc-round3} span a spectrum of algebraic
structure, from unstructured LWE (FrodoKEM~\cite{takeofffrodo}), through cyclotomic modules (\kyber~\cite{kyberoriginal} and
Saber~\cite{AFRICACRYPT:DKRV18}), to prime-degree number fields (NTRU LPRime~\cite{ntruprime}), reflecting a trade-off between efficiency
and conservatism. ML-KEM's module structure gives small keys and fast NTT arithmetic, but is also seen as a potential attack surface,
which other designs avoid entirely; FrodoKEM, for example, rests on plain LWE, one reason it was recommended by BSI, ENISA, and
NLNCSA~\cite{frodokem-news} and recently standardized by ISO~\cite{frodoiso}.

\paragraph{Structure, margin, and diversity.}The efficiency-vs.-conservatism framing bundles together two notions of conservatism that concern different classes of attack.
The first arises from the choice of hardness assumption: FrodoKEM is grounded on unstructured LWE to avoid
a hypothetical attack that would exploit the algebraic structure of ring/module LWE but not apply directly to plain LWE.
The second is the security margin a scheme carries above its target level, which protects against advances in the best known attacks,
such as improved lattice reduction, dual attacks, or sharper cost models. Such advances would affect the concrete security of every
lattice scheme at its current parameters, including FrodoKEM.
A third, orthogonal hedge is to change the hardness 
assumption, as HQC does by relying on QCSD instead of LWE. This provides diversity, and at first glance, some additional assurance
since a break of LWE could still leave HQC secure. However, it is not entirely certain that the two hardness assumptions are cryptanalytically independent.
Recent work has shown connections between lattice and code-based cryptanalysis, 
including adaptations of lattice-sieving techniques to codes~\cite{EC:DEEK24}. The quasi-cyclic structure of HQC has also been noted to 
have important parallels with the ring and module structures used in structured lattice schemes~\cite{hqc-forum}.

\paragraph{Security margin.}Of all these hedges, increasing the margin has the longest proven track record. For computationally secure schemes, a concrete security estimate is the cost
of the best attack known at the time, and such estimates evolve as cryptanalysis matures. The standard response has been to increase
parameters whenever bandwidth allows; for instance, the recommended size of an RSA modulus climbed from $512$ to $1024$ to $2048$ bits and beyond
as factoring algorithms and available computation advanced~\cite{nist-sp800-57pt1r5}. Lattice estimates are not static either.
The attacks and the cost models used to estimate them have evolved over more than a decade~\cite{AC:CheNgu11,estimator}, and while the
underlying framework has remained stable, with BKZ-based lattice reduction at its core, the cost of the best known attacks continues
to drift in both directions as improvements are proposed~\cite{AC:GuoJoh21,matzov2022lwe}, scrutinized~\cite{C:DucPul23}, and
refined~\cite{C:CMST25}. This drift affects structured and unstructured schemes alike, and its impact depends on how much margin is available to accommodate it. 
However, additional margin also comes at a cost, requiring larger parameters and hence higher bandwidth and runtime, 
so no scheme can withstand advances in cryptanalysis indefinitely simply by scaling its parameters. For LWE-family KEMs there is a further constraint, since the parameters 
that determine security also control the decryption failure probability, so increasing the noise for higher lattice security necessarily affects correctness.

\paragraph{Combining schemes.}Since margin comes at a growing cost, and it is impossible to predict which of the (hypothetical) breakthroughs above is most likely,
a natural response is also to combine schemes.
Hybrid key exchange, which combines two KEMs so that an attacker has to break both~\cite{PKC:GiaHeuPoe18,PQCRYPTO:BBFGS19},
is already standard practice in the post-quantum transition, where it typically pairs a post-quantum KEM with a classical one,
as in the X25519+ML-KEM combinations now deployed in TLS~\cite{tls-hybrid}. There, the classical component is a safety net, as
post-quantum schemes are young and have received far less cryptanalytic scrutiny than their classical counterparts.
Against a quantum adversary, however, the classical component offers no protection, so for long-term confidentiality the combination
is only as strong as its post-quantum half. This same principle extends naturally to combining two post-quantum KEMs, each covering
a different failure mode.

\paragraph{Beyond FrodoKEM-1344.}Of the standalone schemes available today, FrodoKEM-1344 comes closest to covering both the structure and margin hedges, as it avoids algebraic
structure entirely and carries the largest security estimate among the standardized lattice KEMs. It is also by far the most expensive, with public
keys and ciphertexts of over 21\,KB each, more than an order of magnitude larger than those of ML-KEM-1024~\cite{takeofffrodo,fips203}.
This cost reflects the fact that structure determines the \emph{price} of buying margin. At matched security, an unstructured scheme already
needs roughly an order of magnitude more bandwidth and considerably more computation than a module scheme~\cite{takeofffrodo,kyberoriginal}.
Within FrodoKEM's communication budget, a structured scheme can therefore afford far larger parameters, and, combined in a hybrid with a
smaller FrodoKEM parameter set, potentially cover both hedges more cheaply than FrodoKEM-1344 alone.

\paragraph{Scaling ML-KEM.}The natural way to obtain such a module scheme is to scale ML-KEM itself, whose parameter sets already
differ mainly in their module rank $k$, so it allows lattice security to be adjusted by changing $k$
while reusing the same ring arithmetic~\cite{kyberoriginal}. Recent work has explored the analogous high-margin regime for unstructured LWE in the form of KyFrog~\cite{kyfrog}, which increases
the LWE dimension and adjusts the noise distribution to reach substantially higher security estimates at the cost of much larger
ciphertexts. What has received less attention is how far module rank can be pushed before the correctness limit discussed above
takes over, and what it costs to push past it. We therefore ask:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a scaled ML-KEM remain correct and efficient within FrodoKEM's tightest communication budget, and, combined with FrodoKEM in a hybrid,
provide a better hedge than FrodoKEM-1344?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Our approach.}We answer both parts affirmatively through \kaiburr\footnote{\url{https://starwars.fandom.com/wiki/Kaiburr_crystal}}, which increases the module rank $k$ in ML-KEM, replaces its centered binomial distribution with a
generalized one-parameter family that remains easy to sample, and removes ciphertext compression. The ring,
NTT arithmetic, symmetric primitives, and FO transform are unchanged, so existing optimized ML-KEM software and hardware can largely be
reused. We instantiate \kaiburr up to lattice dimension $6144$, reaching $1717$ bits of classical Core-SVP security within
FrodoKEM-640's communication budget, and show that a hybrid of \kaiburr with FrodoKEM hedges against both structural attacks
and improvements to lattice attacks at lower cost than FrodoKEM-1344.

\paragraph{Our contributions.}
We make several contributions in this work, summarized below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A generalized noise distribution that lifts the correctness limit.} Raising $k$ alone, with \kyber's noise and
    modulus fixed, keeps decryption failure below $2^{-128}$ only up to $k=7$ (\cref{sec:warmup}). We therefore introduce
    $f(t)$, a one-parameter family that generalizes \kyber's centered binomial distribution and 
    retains simple constant-time sampling. Tuning $t$ against
    $k$ keeps decryption failure below $2^{-128}$ as $k$ grows (\cref{sec:distribution}).
  \item \emph{Concrete parameter sets with large margins within FrodoKEM-640's communication budget.} We obtain three parameter sets,
    \kaiburr-1792, -4608, and -6144, at $k=7,18,24$, whose classical/quantum Core-SVP security climbs from $479/434$ to $1717/1557$
    bits, while their public keys and ciphertexts remain smaller than FrodoKEM-640's (\cref{sec:distribution}).
  \item \emph{A formally verified bound on decryption failure.} Building on the EasyCrypt framework of~\cite{CCS:BKLSS25}, we show
    that removing ciphertext compression makes this bound provable, closing the gap between provable and heuristic bounds that
    compression causes for ML-KEM (\cref{sec:verification}).
  \item \emph{Implementations across three targets.} Since \kaiburr keeps ML-KEM's ring, NTT arithmetic, and symmetric
    primitives, existing implementation techniques carry over. We implement all three parameter sets in C, in Jasmin with
    machine-checked constant-time verification, and as an ACC variant targeting the Pavona hardware root of trust, and describe the
    engineering consequences of scaling $k$ and removing ciphertext compression (\cref{sec:implementation}).
  \item \emph{Benchmarks against FrodoKEM and HQC.} Across three Intel microarchitectures, our C and Jasmin AVX2 implementations run
    substantially faster than FrodoKEM-640 and hence the larger FrodoKEM parameter sets. \kaiburr-1792 outperforms HQC-5 in every operation. Against HQC-1/3/5 at matching
    indices, HQC is faster in key generation, and in encapsulation for the two larger parameter sets, while decapsulation, HQC's
    most expensive operation, favors \kaiburr (\cref{sec:benchmarks}).
  \item \emph{\kaiburr--FrodoKEM hybrids instead of high-security FrodoKEM parameter sets.} Our results call into question
    the use of FrodoKEM's larger parameter sets. We show that, combined with FrodoKEM-640 or
    FrodoKEM-976, \kaiburr is faster than the next-larger FrodoKEM parameter set. For example, the hybrid of \kaiburr-1792 and
    FrodoKEM-976 is smaller and about $1.7\times$ faster than FrodoKEM-1344 and has a much higher lattice attack cost ($2^{467.3}$
    vs.\ $2^{281.7}$) (\cref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
